From a listed comparator to a discount rate: how much is a long stream of cash worth?

An unlisted asset has no share price and so no beta. Valuers borrow one from listed companies that look similar, strip out the effect of their borrowing to get the risk of the assets themselves, put back the borrowing the asset will actually carry, and add the cost of debt to make a weighted average cost of capital. The value of a long stream of cash is then very sensitive to that rate, and more so the longer the stream. Set the comparator and the asset, and see what the rate does to value.

RE07 Global Infrastructure Investment and Management, Week 6. The capital asset pricing and weighted-cost arithmetic is textbook (asset beta from the comparator's equity beta, gearing and debt beta; relevered at the target gearing without tax; cost of equity equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium plus any extra premium; weighted cost with the target gearing); I derived and checked it, and the page confirms that the weighted cost equals the unlevered cost plus the debt's own premium. The regulatory setting is the Competition and Markets Authority's final determination of March 2026 on the allowed return in water, as set out in Table 2.1 of Oxera (27 March 2026), page 2: gearing 55 per cent, risk-free rate 2.40 per cent, equity beta 0.66, total market return 6.95 per cent, return on equity 5.70 per cent, return on debt 2.97 per cent, appointee weighted cost 4.20 per cent, all in CPIH-real terms. The model's capital asset pricing cost of equity is 5.40 per cent on those inputs, so the preset adds 0.30 points to reach the CMA's 5.70, which is the CMA's choice of a point in its range; I did not read the CMA's report. The extra premium stands for the cost of not being able to sell, which Ang, Papanikolaou and Westerfield (NBER Working Paper 19436, 2013) analyse; I use it as a user input and take no value from the paper. The beta of 0.5 and 1.0 for listed data-centre property companies come from Van Nieuwerburgh (NBER Working Paper 35865, 2026), Figure 3, read by eye. Nothing else is calibrated to any asset. The model was computed twice, in Python and in this page, and the two agree on 300 random settings.

What the model says

A valuer has to choose a discount rate for a stream of cash with no price to test it against. The usual route is to find listed firms whose business looks like the asset's, read their equity betas, remove the effect of their gearing to get the risk of the underlying business, and then put on the gearing the asset will carry. A more indebted firm has a riskier equity, so the equity beta rises with gearing, but debt is cheaper, so the weighted cost of capital changes much less. The first chart shows the parts: a risk-free rate, a premium that is the beta times the market's, an optional extra premium, and the cost of debt weighted in at the target gearing.

The second chart shows why the choice matters. The value of a cash flow that runs for many years is roughly one divided by the gap between the discount rate and the growth rate, so a rate that is one point lower can raise the value by a fifth or more, and the longer the stream the larger the rise. A listed comparator is no help if its beta is unstable. The third chart varies the comparator's beta, and the presets show a case that is current: listed data-centre property companies had a beta of about 0.5 from 2021 to 2023 and of about 1 through 2024 and 2025 (Van Nieuwerburgh, 2026), though it fell back at the last observation, and on the inputs of the two presets that moves the weighted cost of capital from 6.35 to 8.10 per cent and the value of a 25-year cash flow by about 16 per cent. The valuer's answer to a question of what an asset is worth is therefore, in part, an answer to a question about which period's beta to believe.

The regulatory preset shows the same machinery in official use. The CMA's decision for the water companies used a gearing of 55 per cent, a risk-free rate of 2.40 per cent and an equity beta of 0.66, and arrived at a weighted cost of 4.20 per cent on the wholesale basis, 23 basis points above Ofwat's own final determination (market data to 30 September 2024), the difference coming from how the inputs were measured and when. The extra premium is where an investor who cannot sell puts a price on that. The page does not say what the premium should be.

The model ignores tax and the value of the tax shield of debt, takes the debt beta as given, and treats a cash flow that grows at a fixed rate as the only possible profile. A real valuation would use a forecast, a terminal value and a range of rates. The CMA's own estimate of the cost of equity involved choices of range and point that the capital asset pricing model alone does not reproduce, which is why the preset needs its extra 0.30 points.
