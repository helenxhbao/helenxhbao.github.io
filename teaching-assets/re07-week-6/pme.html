Beating the index: does a fund with a high multiple outperform the market?

An unlisted fund reports an internal rate of return and a multiple of the capital invested. Neither says whether the investor did better than if the same money had gone into an index, because the money was called and returned at particular dates, and the index did different things on those dates. Kaplan and Schoar (2005) proposed the public market equivalent: compound every contribution and every distribution at the index's return and compare the two totals. Gredil, Griffiths and Stucke turn the same flows into an annual excess return, the direct alpha. Set the fund's multiple and its timetable, set what the index did, and see whether the fund beat it.

The public market equivalent is defined in Kaplan and Schoar (2005), Journal of Finance 60(4), 1791–1823, which I read as the NBER conference draft of 6 April 2003, pages 8 to 9 (printed pages): distributions and contributions, net of fees, valued at the total return on the index, and a ratio above one means the fund outperformed. The direct alpha is the internal rate of return of the cash flows after each has been compounded to the end date at the index's return, as described by Gredil, Griffiths and Stucke (2014 working paper; I have not read the paper and rely on two secondary descriptions). The fund here is stylised and generic: one unit of capital is called in each of the first years, and the distributions are equal amounts over a window of years. The benchmark can be scaled by a beta, as the 2003 draft suggests on page 9. Nothing on the page is calibrated to a real fund. The model was computed twice, in Python and in this page, and the two agree on 300 random funds.

What the model says

An investor in an unlisted fund puts money in over several years and gets it back over several more. Comparing the fund's internal rate of return with the index's return over the period compares two different things, because the fund's money was not all invested for the whole period. The public market equivalent removes the difficulty by asking what each contribution would have grown to, and each distribution would have been worth, had it been invested in the benchmark. If the benchmark value of what came back exceeds the benchmark value of what went in, the fund beat the benchmark, and the ratio is by how much.

Two things in the result are worth noticing. First, the ratio depends on when the index did well. A fund that returns capital after a long boom has been compared with an index that has already risen, so the same cash flows give a lower ratio, and when the index gains late the same flows look better. Second, the answer depends on what the benchmark is. Kaplan and Schoar note that if the fund's market beta is above one, the ratio overstates its risk-adjusted performance, and the third control lets you raise the benchmark's beta to see how quickly a good number can disappear. The direct alpha gives the same verdict as the ratio, positive when the ratio is above one, but expresses it as an annual rate that can be set against a fee.

For infrastructure funds the method is only as good as the benchmark. There is no listed index of unlisted infrastructure, the funds differ in how much they borrow, and the final unrealised value, which enters the last cash flow of a fund that has not been wound up, is the manager's estimate. The page assumes a fully realised fund. That is the best case for the method.
