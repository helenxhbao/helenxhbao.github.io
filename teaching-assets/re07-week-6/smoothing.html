Smooth reported returns: what happens to risk, Sharpe ratio and beta when values lag prices?

An asset that is not traded every day is valued by an appraiser, a model or the last transaction, and the valuation moves more slowly than the price would. Getmansky, Lo and Makarov (2004) wrote the simplest model of this: the reported return in a period is a weighted average of the true return in that period and in the few before it. The average return is unchanged. The volatility falls, the Sharpe ratio rises, the measured beta on the market shrinks towards zero, and part of the market's effect turns up in later periods. True returns follow a one-factor model with a market beta, the reported return is the weighted average of the current and k lagged true returns with weights summing to one, and the three weight profiles (equal, declining by the sum of the years' digits, geometric with a decay of one half) are mine. The desmoothing divides out a first-order filter, in the way real-estate analysts desmooth appraisal series (the usual reference is Geltner; I did not read it), with the first-order autocorrelation of the reported series as the filter coefficient. It is exact when the smoothing is first-order and otherwise only approximate. What the model says

Suppose that the market value of a stake moves every quarter, but the reported value is a weighted average of the last few quarters' true changes. Then everything the true series contains does eventually appear, so the long-run average return is the same, and so is the volatility over a horizon much longer than the lag. What changes is the short run. Each reported change is an average of several true ones, so it is less variable, and it is correlated with the one before because the two share quarters. The first chart shows the effect: the reported series is flatter and slower than the true one.

Three consequences follow, and all of them flatter the asset. The volatility measured from reported returns is lower than the true volatility by the square root of the sum of the squared weights. The Sharpe ratio, which divides an excess return that has not changed by a volatility that has fallen, is higher by the reciprocal. And the beta on the market in the same quarter is the true beta times the weight on the current quarter, so an asset that moves one for one with the market looks, on a regression of same-quarter returns, as if it had a beta of 0.4 or less. The market's effect is not missing. It is reported later: the third chart shows that the betas on the market's lagged returns add back up to the true beta. A regression that includes lags of the market recovers it.

For unlisted infrastructure the model supplies the arithmetic behind a familiar puzzle: indices built from appraisals show volatility of a few per cent and betas near zero, which would make them the best diversifier ever found. Lambrev (2019) reports exactly that for the EDHECinfra index of unlisted infrastructure against listed infrastructure indices. The model says to treat such numbers as properties of the valuation process before treating them as properties of the asset, and it says how much to adjust them, given a guess at the lag.

The model has one source of smoothing with known fixed weights. Real appraisal lags vary with the market, the appraiser and the date, and appraisers also respond to the news, which the model does not allow. The Sharpe ratios use the square-root-of-time scaling of the paper's per-period results.
