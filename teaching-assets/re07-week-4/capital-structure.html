Debt, tax shields and default costs: how much should a project borrow?

Debt is cheaper than equity partly because interest is tax deductible, and every extra unit of debt raises the chance that the project defaults and loses value to lawyers, delay and fire sales. Leland (1994) puts the two together in a model where the equity holders decide when to stop paying, and finds the amount of debt that maximises the value of the whole firm. Set the asset's risk and the cost of failure and see how much debt it carries, at what spread, and why a low-risk asset can borrow more.

What the model says

The asset's value drifts and shakes. The firm borrows against it with a permanent coupon, deducts the interest from tax while it pays, and stops paying when the equity is worth more dead than alive. Equity holders choose that moment, so the default boundary falls as the coupon falls and rises with the coupon. A higher coupon buys more tax shield but moves the boundary closer to today's value and so raises the present value of the default cost. The best coupon balances the two, and the page reports what the firm looks like there.

Two things move the answer most. Risk makes the boundary easier to hit, so less debt is worth carrying, and the cost of failure scales the penalty. Project finance structures work on the second. A special purpose vehicle with step-in rights, a single asset and contracted revenue is meant to make failure cheap and cash flow predictable, which together are what the model needs for high leverage. The model's leverage at generic settings is below the 90 per cent the EDHEC Infrastructure Institute reports for projects with little market risk, and the gap is a prompt to ask which input a real case would change.

With V the asset value, r the risk-free rate, δ the payout rate, σ the volatility, τ the tax rate and α the loss in default, let λ be the positive root of ½σ²λ² + (½σ² − r + δ)λ − r = 0. The boundary is VB = kC with k = λ(1 − τ)/[r(1 + λ)], the probability weight of default is P = (VB/V)λ, debt is D = (C/r)(1 − P) + (1 − α)VBP, and firm value is v = V + (τC/r)(1 − P) − αVBP. The best coupon has P* = τ/[(1 + λ)(τ + αrk)] and C* = (V/k)P*1/λ

The model has one asset, permanent debt and no distinction between senior and junior claims, and it takes tax and default costs as the only frictions. Real project debt is amortising, and its cost is also driven by covenants, reserve accounts and the tenant's or regulator's credit. The numbers on this page are not calibrated and should be read as comparisons, not predictions.
