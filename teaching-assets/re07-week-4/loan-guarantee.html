A loan guarantee as an option: what is a promise to pay worth?

A government, a development bank or a tenant that guarantees a project's loan promises to pay the lender whatever the project cannot. Merton (1977) saw that this promise has the payoff of a put option on the project's value, with the loan as the strike, and so has a price that can be computed before anyone is called on to pay. The price rises with the loan-to-value ratio and with the asset's volatility, and it is higher than the probability-weighted cost a budget would record. Set the loan and the asset and see what the guarantee is worth, what it saves the borrower, and what share of the tail it covers.

What the model says

If the project is worth more than the loan at maturity the lender is repaid and the guarantor pays nothing. If it is worth less the guarantor pays the shortfall, up to the share it has covered. That is the payoff of a put option struck at the loan, and a partial guarantee is a put spread. Its price is the cost of replicating the payoff by trading the underlying asset, and it grows with the loan, because the strike rises, and with volatility, because the tail fattens. The lender, who is relieved of the same exposure, will accept a lower interest rate. The saving in the rate and the price of the guarantee are two views of the same quantity.

A budget that records a guarantee when it is called, or that books the probability-weighted cost, sees only the second figure on the page. The market value is higher because the guarantor is exposed in states of the world, such as a downturn, in which investors value an extra unit of money more, and the gap between the two is the risk premium. Giving a guarantee for free is therefore a transfer to the project of the market value, which is large for a highly geared project, and the same logic applies to a revenue guarantee, a minimum value guarantee on a lease and a tenant's residual value guarantee. Each is a put written by someone with a stronger balance sheet.

With asset value V, loan D, rate r, volatility σ and term T, the guarantee is the put P(D) = De−rTN(−d2) − VN(−d1); a cover of share c is P(D) − P((1 − c)D). The lender's loan is worth De−rT − P(D) without the guarantee. The probability-weighted cost uses the same formula with the real-world growth rate in place of r for the distribution of V, discounted at the risk-free rate

The loan is a single payment at maturity, interim cash flows and covenants are ignored, and the project's value is treated as the value of a tradable asset. Real guarantees also have claims procedures, time limits, caps, fees and a counterparty whose own credit may be doubtful. The comparison between market value and probability-weighted cost depends on the growth rate chosen, which is an assumption and not an estimate.
