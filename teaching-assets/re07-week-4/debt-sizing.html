Sizing debt on a cover ratio: how much room does the lender leave?

Project lenders do not size a loan against the value of the assets. They size it against the cash the project is expected to make: the loan is set so that expected cash available for debt service covers the instalment by a margin, the base-case cover. What the margin buys depends on how far and how persistently cash flow wanders from the base case. The EDHEC Infrastructure Institute documents that wandering for more than 200 projects. Set the margin and the volatility and see the chance that cover drops below one, the point at which the project cannot pay.

RE07 Global Infrastructure Investment and Management, Week 4. The idea of tracking the debt service cover ratio as a stochastic process, and the evidence that realised cover is approximately lognormal, serially correlated, and less volatile for contracted than merchant projects, is from the EDHEC Infrastructure Institute-Singapore (2016), Cash Flow Dynamics of Private Infrastructure Project Debt. The process on this page is my simplification: one Gaussian autoregressive shock to the log of cover around the base case, with the probability of ever falling below a level computed on a grid. It is a teaching illustration with generic inputs, not an estimate for any project. It was computed twice, in Python and in this page, and the two agree; the Python was also checked against a simulation of one million paths.

What the model says

The debt service cover ratio is cash available for debt service divided by the debt service due. When it falls below one the project cannot pay out of its own cash, which the EDHEC study treats as an unambiguous definition of a hard default, and covenants typically bite earlier at a level such as 1.15, locking up distributions to equity. The lender sets the base-case cover so that these thresholds are unlikely to be reached. How unlikely depends on two features of the cash flow, its volatility and its persistence. Persistence cuts two ways. With the overall volatility held fixed, a more persistent process has fewer independent draws over the life of the loan, so the chance of ever reaching a breach from the base case is lower, but a dip, once it comes, lasts longer, which is what gives lenders and sponsors less time to cure it.

The page shows the trade the sponsor faces. A higher cover means a smaller loan, because the same expected cash flow supports less debt, and a lower cover means a larger loan and a higher chance of breach. A contracted project, whose cash flow is largely fixed, can run on a thin margin, while a merchant project needs a thick one, which is why leverage and cover go together in project finance. The distance of the base case from the breach level, measured in standard deviations, is the quantity EDHEC links to the Merton distance to default, and it is the link to the guarantee model on the third page.

One shock drives the whole path, with no separate construction phase, no reserve accounts, no refinancing and no change of regime between a risky and a safe state, which the EDHEC model has. A fall below 1.0 is a breach and not a loss: what lenders recover after a default is a separate question (an ultimate recovery of 84 per cent on unrated infrastructure project finance loans originated from 1983 to 2021, World Bank Group and Global Infrastructure Hub, Infrastructure Monitor 2024, page 18). If the volatility of each year's shock were held fixed instead of the overall volatility, higher persistence would raise the long-run dispersion, and the effect on the breach probability would reverse.
