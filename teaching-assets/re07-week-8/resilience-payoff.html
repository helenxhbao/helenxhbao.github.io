The payoff to resilience

A resilience measure pays for itself by the losses it prevents, and most of those losses come from events that are rare. The value of the measure therefore depends on three things that are hard to know: how large the loss from a rare event is, how fast the frequency of such events is rising, and how the distant benefits are discounted. This page builds the simplest version of that calculation. A curve links the return period of an event to its loss, a protection standard removes the losses below it, and the avoided losses are discounted and compared with the cost. Use it to find the standard at which the net benefit is greatest, and to see how much the answer moves when the climate or the discount rate does.

Hazard, exposure and vulnerability follow the framework of the World Resources Institute’s Aqueduct 4.0 (Kuzma and others), and the sense of scale comes from the World Bank’s Lifelines (Hallegatte, Rentschler and Rozenberg, 2019). 1. The hazard and the loss

Loss from an event of a given return period

Loss from an event with a return period of T years is the 100-year loss times (T/100) to the power of this number. A high value means the rare events are far worse than the common ones.

Events of every size become this share more frequent each year, so after 40 years at 0.5 per cent they are 20 per cent more frequent.

2. The measure

Protects against every event up to its standard

Cost is the one-in-20 cost times (standard/20) to this power. At 1 the cost doubles each time the standard doubles.

What the model says

Let p be the probability in a year of an event at least as bad as a given one, so an event with return period T has p = 1/T. The loss curve is L(T) = L100(T/100)s. The expected annual loss is the area under the loss against the probability, which has a closed form because the curve is a power. Events more frequent than one in two years and rarer than one in a thousand years are left out. A measure with a standard of R years removes the losses of every event with a return period up to R, so the loss it avoids is the part of the area to the right of 1/R. Events beyond the standard are unchanged, and the shaded area in the first chart is what it protects.

In Aqueduct’s terms the loss at a given return period is the product of the hazard, the value exposed and the vulnerability of what is exposed, and the page folds the last two into the loss itself (Kuzma and others, Aqueduct 4.0). Climate change enters as a rise in the frequency of events of every size and growth enters as a rise in the value exposed, so the avoided loss in year t is today’s avoided loss times (1 + γt)(1 + e)t. The present value of benefits discounts these at the chosen regime and the present value of costs is the capital cost plus a share of it each year.

Three results are worth reading off. The net benefit rises and then falls with the standard, because the first increments of protection remove frequent small losses cheaply and the later ones remove rare large losses at a cost that rises faster than the loss avoided. The discount regime matters most where the benefit arrives late, so the fourth statistic above, the share of the benefit that falls after year 30, is a guide to how much the answer depends on the schedule. The drift in frequency and exposure raises the benefit and also postpones it, so that it counts for more at low discount rates. The Lifelines study finds benefit-cost ratios for resilient infrastructure above 1 in 96 per cent of its 3,000 scenarios and above 6 in 25 per cent, and that climate change doubles the median ratio (page 12 of the overview). Those are results from a global study with its own assumptions. They are a reference for the range, not a test of this page.

The curve has no uncertainty about its own shape, no interaction between events and no failure of the measure beyond its standard. The result is a ceiling on what a real appraisal would find. The benefit counts avoided losses only and leaves out any benefit the measure delivers in ordinary years.
