Discounting long-lived assets

A road, a sea wall or a water network pays back over decades, so the rate at which the future is discounted decides whether it looks worth building. Most of the value of a long-lived asset arrives after the point at which a few tenths of a percentage point have compounded into a factor of two. This page puts the UK Treasury’s declining schedule, its sensitivity case, the two schedules recommended by the June 2026 independent review, a flat rate and an uncertain rate side by side. Use it to see how much the answer depends on the choice, and where the dependence is greatest.

What the model says

The Treasury sets the rate at which public spending is discounted from the Ramsey rule, the social time preference rate r = δ + L + μg. The rate has three parts: pure time preference δ of 0.5 per cent, an allowance L of 1 per cent for catastrophic risk, and a wealth effect, the elasticity of marginal utility μ of 1 times expected growth in consumption per head g of 2 per cent. They sum to 3.5 per cent (Green Book supplementary guidance, paragraphs 2.1 and 2.2, page 7; review paragraph 7, page 4 of 15). Because the future values of those parameters are uncertain, the guidance lowers the rate with the horizon, to 3.0 per cent for years 31 to 75 and 2.5 per cent for years 76 to 125 (paragraph 3.1 and Table 3.A, page 12). Beyond 50 years it also asks for a sensitivity run with pure time preference removed (paragraphs 3.3 to 3.5, pages 12 and 13).

The review of June 2026 recommends a flatter schedule, 3.0 per cent falling to 2.25 per cent, because the standard model of consumption growth cannot generate a strongly declining rate from the historical data and only structural uncertainty about the growth regime does (paragraphs 44 to 46, page 10 of 15; Table 1, page 2). It adds a second schedule, 1.5 per cent falling to 0.75 per cent, for projects with significant social insurance properties, meaning interventions against severe downside risks to society whose benefits arrive when marginal utility is high, with a risk premium of −0.5 per cent (paragraph 60, page 12 of 15). Whether a resilience measure counts as one is a judgement that the review leaves to HM Treasury, and this page does not decide it.

The gamma case is the textbook way to see why uncertainty about the rate lowers the long-run rate. If the right rate r is gamma distributed with mean μ and standard deviation σ, the expected discount factor at year t is (1 + θt)−k, with k = μ2/σ2 and θ = σ2/μ. The rate applied to year t is then μ/(1 + σ2t/μ), which starts at the mean and falls with the horizon, because the low-rate states dominate the average at long horizons. Weitzman (2001) made the argument with a survey of economists; the formulas here are a derivation from the gamma moment generating function.

Read the third and fourth charts together. At the default settings the value today of a single amount in year 100 differs by a factor of about ten between the flat 3.5 per cent and the social insurance schedule, while the benefit-cost ratio of a level stream differs by a factor of about one and a half, because much of a level stream arrives in the early years where the regimes agree. A project whose benefits are concentrated late, as the avoided losses of rare events are, is the case in which the choice of schedule matters most.

The guidance and the review do not agree on one point that bears on this page. The review recommends that discount-rate sensitivity analysis not be undertaken (page 2), and the February 2026 guidance requires it for horizons beyond 50 years (paragraph 3.4). Which stands is a matter for HM Treasury.

Set the single amount to year 100 and read the value today under the Green Book, the review’s standard schedule and its social insurance schedule. Report the ratio of the largest to the smallest and say what it means for a project whose benefit is an avoided loss a century away.

Raise the standard deviation of the uncertain rate from 1 to 3 per cent at a mean of 3 per cent. Report the forward rate in year 100 and explain why the rate of the gamma regime falls with the horizon while the flat rate does not.
