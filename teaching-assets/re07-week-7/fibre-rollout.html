How many customers must a fibre network connect before it pays?

A fibre network spends most of its money before it has a customer: it digs, lays and lights the street, and only then waits for households to sign up and be connected. The return therefore depends on three things the builder cannot fully control: what the street costs to pass, how many of the homes passed take the service and how soon, and what price the wholesale market will bear. This page takes the cost items that Ofcom uses for a reasonably efficient entrant and asks what monthly price per connected line just recovers them, and how that price moves with take-up, with the cost of capital and with a second network in the same streets.

The cost items are Ofcom's Fibre Cost Model as published in the Telecoms Access Review 2026-31 (17 March 2026): for a reasonably efficient entrant in the competitive area (WLA Area 2) a build cost of £307.43 (low) to £465.91 (high) per premises passed, a connection cost of £330.94 to £383.28 per connection, operating costs of £51.66 to £54.56 per customer a year, access to Openreach's ducts (PIA) of £12.40 to £18.90 per line (which I read as pounds a year), take-up of 40% to 30% five years after build, and a pre-tax nominal cost of capital of 9.0% to 10.3% from April 2026 (Annex 11, Tables A11.5 and A11.6, page 233, and paragraph A11.154, page 231). Ofcom's published result is that such an entrant would have to charge £13.30 to £20.76 a month in 2025/26 prices (A11.161, page 233). The page's discount rate is real: I take Ofcom's pre-tax nominal rate less 2 per cent inflation, which is my assumption. The take-up path (a straight line to the final level over the years you choose, then flat), the 40-year horizon and the absence of any replacement of electronics are mine; Ofcom models electronics lives of 10 years and passive lives of 20. On Ofcom's own inputs the page gives £12.69 and £21.55 against Ofcom's £13.30 and £20.76. The Area 3 presets use Ofcom's Annex 12: build £734 per premises passed (central), connection £554 plus £25 lead-to-cash, other operating costs £58 per customer a year, take-up of 90% for Openreach where no altnet is present, 60% where one is, and 30% for the altnet, and a price of £18.18 (the cap on FTTP 80/20 at the start of the control period). Nothing else is calibrated to any operator. The model was computed twice, in Python and in this page, and the two agree on 300 random settings.

What the model says

The model asks for one number, the price per connected line per month at which the discounted flows of the network sum to zero. The build cost is paid for every premises passed, whether or not anyone connects, so the cost of each connected customer is the build cost divided by the share of homes that connect, plus the connection cost, plus the running cost, all spread over the years in which the customer pays. That is why the second chart is steep at low take-up: the price needed to recover a build cost of a few hundred pounds a home from one home in five is several times the price needed when two homes in three connect.

Take-up builds over years, and the evidence of how fast is in Ofcom's Connected Nations 2025: of the properties that have had full fibre for a year or less, 14 per cent have taken it; after two years, 30 per cent; after three, 41 per cent; and after three to four years or more, 55 per cent (Table 2.6, page 12). Across all networks 42 per cent of premises that can get full fibre have taken it. The ramp slider sets how many years the straight-line path takes to reach the final level.

The third chart is the one that changes how the investment looks. In the area where Openreach has built alone, Ofcom assumes that it reaches 90 per cent take-up; where an alternative network also passes the homes, Openreach reaches 60 per cent and the alternative 30 (Annex 12, paragraphs A12.34 and A12.35 and footnote 599, page 243). The total number of customers is the same; they are shared. Because the build cost is the same whoever the customers choose, the second network does not add customers, it divides them, and the break-even price of each network rises. On Ofcom's central Area 3 costs the price that just covers the cost is about £13.35 for a network that has the area to itself, £16.11 for one that keeps two thirds of the customers, and £24.39 for one that has a third, against a regulated price of £18.18. The first earns well above its cost of capital at that price, the second a little above it, and the third does not cover its cost. The page does not say what is efficient; it shows who bears the cost of duplicating a network.

Ofcom's model is more detailed than this one: it phases the roll-out over years, models infrastructure reuse, replaces electronics, has take-up that continues to rise, and sets the price in real terms against nominal capital costs. Where the page's output differs from Ofcom's, Ofcom's is the better estimate. The page is a way of seeing which assumptions matter, and the one that matters most is take-up. It treats customers as shared in proportion to a given share, which is a simplification of how a second network competes: in practice the price, the quality and the timing of the second network would also change the shares.

Start from the low-cost entrant and raise take-up from 40 to 55 per cent, the level Ofcom reports for properties with full fibre for three years or more. Report the fall in the break-even price, and say what the answer implies for an investor who has to decide how much to pay for a network that is only two years old.

Choose the Area 3 preset for Openreach alone and move the share of customers to 67 per cent, then to 33 per cent. Report the break-even price each time, compare it with the price of £18.18, and say who pays for a duplicate network when the regulated price does not rise.
