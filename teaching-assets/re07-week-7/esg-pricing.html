Pricing sustainability preferences

If some investors care how green their holdings are, the assets they like become dearer and the assets they dislike become cheaper. The greener asset then offers a lower expected return, and the investors who care hold more of it. Pastor, Stambaugh and Taylor (2021) derive this in a few lines from the standard mean-variance model. Use it to see how large the price effect is, who earns the lower return, and why the effect needs investors to disagree.

The assumptions behind the assets

Illustrative. The risk-free rate is 3 per cent. The expected return on the market is set by the model, a times its variance (equation 7).

What the model says

Each investor holds a mix of the assets and gets a benefit di gn from each unit of asset n with ESG score gn. Prices adjust until the markets clear. The result is that the expected excess return on asset n is the CAPM return less a term proportional to its score, so the CAPM alpha is −(d̄/a) gn, where d̄ is the wealth-weighted average taste (Proposition 1 and Corollary 2, equations 9 and 10, pages 8 and 9). A greener asset has a negative alpha, a browner one a positive alpha. Both come from the price, not from any news about the assets.

The portfolios follow from the same algebra. An investor whose taste is above the average tilts towards the green assets by (δi/a2) times the inverse covariance matrix times the scores, where δi = di − d̄ (Proposition 3, equation 14, page 10). An investor whose taste is below the average tilts the other way, which includes an investor with no taste at all. So “I do not care about ESG, so I hold the market” is not optimal when others care. If every investor has the same taste, every investor holds the market and the price effect remains (Corollary 4, page 11). The third chart shows the cost of the tilt. The investors who like green give up expected return, and the indifferent investors earn it, in proportion to the dispersion of tastes.

The model is a statement about expected returns in a competitive market. It is not a statement about whether the green asset is better for the world. The social impact of holdings enters the authors’ later sections (page 7), but it does not change prices here. For infrastructure the nearest reading is a lower cost of capital for assets that screen well, at the cost of a lower return for the capital that is screened in.
