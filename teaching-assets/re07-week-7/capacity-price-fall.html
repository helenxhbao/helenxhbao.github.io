When the price of capacity falls every year, what is a cable worth?

A submarine cable is built once, lasts on paper for 25 years, and sells capacity whose price per unit has fallen year after year while the demand for capacity has grown even faster. Revenue is price times the quantity sold, and one of the two is falling. This page takes a stylised cable: demand for its capacity grows from an initial fill, price per unit falls at a steady rate, the cable's capacity is fixed until it is upgraded by changing the equipment at the ends of the cable, and the asset has a finite life. Set the rates of price fall and demand growth, the cost of the upgrades and the life, and see when revenue peaks, whether it falls afterwards, and what the asset is worth.

The two rates that drive the page come from TeleGeography's State of the Network 2026 (Transport Networks findings): used international bandwidth demand grew 45 per cent in 2020 and 29 per cent in 2024, a compound rate of 32 per cent from 2020 and a total of 6.4 petabits a second in 2024 (page 7); the weighted median price of an unprotected 100 gigabit wavelength fell by 11 per cent a year on average across the key routes between the fourth quarters of 2021 and 2024, by 25 per cent a year between Miami and São Paulo, 15 per cent between Johannesburg and London, 6 per cent between Singapore and Tokyo and 2 per cent between Marseille and Singapore (pages 13 and 14). New submarine cables were built at an average cost of over US$2 billion a year over the nine years to 2024, and cables worth over US$14 billion may enter service from 2025 to 2027 (pages 11 and 12). A design life of 25 years is the industry convention for the submerged plant, and the equipment at the cable stations is commonly upgraded several times in that life (SubTel Forum; PTC’24 slides, listed below). The demand growth for this particular cable is a setting of yours: route demand grows by about 29 per cent a year, but a cable shares it with the others that serve the route, so the default is 15. The revenue at full capacity in the first year, the initial fill, the build cost, the operating cost, the size and cost of upgrades and the discount rate are generic and mine, chosen so that the default cable earns a little more than its cost of capital with two upgrades and a little less without them. They are not estimates for any cable. The model was computed twice, in Python and in this page, and the two agree on 300 random settings.

What the model says

Revenue in a year is the price per unit, which has been falling, times the smaller of the demand for the cable's capacity and the capacity it has. While demand is below capacity, revenue grows at about the growth of demand less the fall in price: at 15 per cent growth and an 11 per cent fall, about 2 per cent a year. When demand reaches the capacity of the cable the quantity stops growing, and with no upgrade revenue falls at the rate of the price fall, here 11 per cent a year, for the remainder of the life. The first chart shows that peak and the fall after it, and what an upgrade does: it lets the quantity go on growing, at a cost of a fraction of the original build, so that revenue climbs to a second peak.

Two things follow, and both are about how the technology and the market shape value. The first is that the price of a unit of capacity falls on every route, but unevenly: TeleGeography reports 25 per cent a year between Miami and São Paulo, where new cables were arriving, and 2 per cent between Marseille and Singapore, where cables had been delayed by geopolitics and permitting. A cable's value depends sharply on which of these it is on, as the second chart shows, and on whether the next cable to serve the route is already being built. The second is that the option to upgrade is where much of the value lies, so a cable whose owners can add capacity cheaply, because they own the fibre pairs and the stations, is worth more than one whose capacity is sold to others on long contracts that fix its price. In the cable business that option has moved to the buyers of capacity: content and cloud companies accounted for almost three quarters of international bandwidth demand in 2024 and, with others, own or lead a rising share of new cables.

The third chart takes the life of the asset to its limit. A cable with a 25-year design life that can be retired early for commercial reasons, or a satellite with a life of five years, must recover its cost sooner, and a falling price leaves less time to do so. The point is general: for any digital asset whose product loses price every year, the question is how long the equipment lasts relative to how quickly its output cheapens.

The model treats demand for the cable as a smooth growth path and ignores the arrival of competing cables as an event, except through the price fall. It has one price for all capacity, no sale of capacity in advance (the indefeasible rights of use that fund many cables), no repair costs and no residual value. A real cable's revenue depends on contracts, on who the capacity is sold to and on faults. The page is a way of seeing how the two rates interact with capacity and life, and what an upgrade option is worth.

Start from the default, set the price fall to 25 per cent a year (the Miami to São Paulo rate) and then to 2 per cent (Marseille to Singapore). Report the net present value each time with and without upgrades, and say what an investor should want to know about the next cable on the route.

Take the life down to 5 years with the price fall at 25 per cent and no upgrades. Report the value, and say what it implies for the financing of an asset that is built to last 25 years but sells a product whose price halves every three years.
