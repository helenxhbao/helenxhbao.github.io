What does each extra tenant do to the return on a tower?

A mobile tower costs about the same to build and run whether it carries one operator's antennas or three. The first tenant pays for the land, the structure and the power; the later ones pay mainly for the equipment that is bolted on. That is the economic case for a neutral host, and it explains why tower companies report a tenancy ratio as their main operating measure. This page anchors a one-site model on the first-half 2026 figures of a listed tower company, and asks how margin and return on invested capital change with the number of tenants, how much of that depends on how site costs split between fixed and variable, and what a tenant leaving does.

RE07 Global Infrastructure Investment and Management, Week 7. The anchor is Helios Towers plc, results for the six months to 30 June 2026 (30 July 2026): revenue of US$466.3 million, Adjusted EBITDA of US$257.0 million (a margin of 55 per cent), a return on invested capital of 14.4 per cent on annualised portfolio free cash flow of US$362.9 million and invested capital of US$2,526.8 million, 15,270 sites and 34,455 tenancies, a tenancy ratio of 2.26 (release, pages 1 and 2, and the reconciliation on page 15). Per site, annualised, that is revenue of US$61.1 thousand, EBITDA of US$33.7 thousand, free cash flow of US$23.8 thousand and invested capital of US$165.5 thousand; the revenue per tenancy of US$27.1 thousand is revenue per site divided by the tenancy ratio, and the US$9.9 thousand taken from EBITDA before free cash flow is the difference, which covers maintenance and corporate capital spending, lease payments and tax. The company does not say how its site operating costs divide between a part fixed per site and a part that rises with each tenant. What the model says

At the anchor the page reproduces what the company reports: a margin of 55 per cent and a return on invested capital of 14.4 per cent at 2.26 tenants per tower. Away from the anchor, revenue rises in proportion to the number of tenants, but costs do not: the fixed part of the site's cost is paid once, and only the variable part grows with each tenant. Each extra tenant therefore adds most of its revenue to EBITDA, and since the capital in the site is already spent, to the return on invested capital as well. On the default assumption each additional tenant adds about US$21 thousand of EBITDA to a site on which the capital employed is about US$165 thousand, which is roughly thirteen percentage points of return. The first chart shows how steep that is. The same arithmetic runs the other way: a site that loses a tenant loses revenue and saves little cost.

The tenant-loss case is not hypothetical. IHS Holding reported for the second quarter of 2026 a colocation rate of 1.47 on 37,672 towers, with 3,704 tenants lost to churn over the year, 2,576 of them in the third quarter of 2025 under an updated agreement with T2, the smallest key customer in Nigeria, and with MTN Nigeria having agreed in 2024 to vacate about 1,050 sites; its adjusted EBITDA fell 1.3 per cent while revenue rose 10.4 per cent. Cellnex reports the same mechanism in the other direction: in 2025 its points of presence rose 4.5 per cent, from gross colocations of 3.6 per cent and build-to-suit of 2.2 per cent, less churn of 1.2 per cent (the components are rounded), and its portfolio-average tenancy ratio rose about 2 per cent.

The company's own evidence on the return to additions fits the page. Helios reports a return of 14.4 per cent on all its invested capital, but says that the organic growth capital it spent from 2023 to 2025 (US$316 million) added US$112 million to annual portfolio free cash flow, a return of over 30 per cent, and that the extra US$35 million it added to 2026 spending, for about 500 more tenancies of which about 250 are new sites, should add US$10 million a year to EBITDA and free cash flow, about 30 per cent (presentation, pages 7 and 13). The gap between the 14.4 per cent average and the 30 per cent on additions is operating leverage. What the page cannot tell you is how much of the tenancy growth is colocation on existing towers and how much is new towers, and that is the main question to ask a tower company.

The anchor is a company in Africa and the Middle East whose costs include power, much of which is passed through to tenants; margins in India, Europe and China are not comparable, and the page makes no claim about them. Annualising a half year and dividing by period-end sites are simplifications. The model has no new-build capital, no lease escalators and no cost of capital: the return on invested capital is a free cash flow yield on a book base and is not the return to an investor who paid a price for the portfolio.

Start from the anchor and take the tenancy ratio down to 1.5, as it would be if one of the three tenants at many sites left. Report the margin and the return on invested capital, change the fixed share from 50 to 80 per cent, and say why the answer depends on a number the company does not report.

Set the cost of adding a tenant to 25 and then to 60 thousand dollars and compare the return on an added tenant with the company's reported return of about 30 per cent on additions. Say what that comparison implies about how many of the additions are new towers.
