Why estimates of the return to infrastructure fall as the evidence improves

Aschauer (1989) found that a ten per cent rise in public capital goes with a four per cent rise in output, an elasticity of 0.39. Calderón, Moral-Benito and Servén (2015) find 0.07 to 0.10 using data for 88 countries over 1960 to 2000. This page applies a dozen simple ways of estimating the same elasticity to their data. Click a row to see what the specification does and why the number moves.

How to read the page

Every row asks the same question of the same data: when a country has more infrastructure per worker, how much more output per worker does it have? The rows differ in what they compare. Levels compare countries, or one country with its own average, and so pick up everything that makes rich countries rich. Changes compare a country with itself a few years earlier, which removes fixed differences between countries but leaves the problem that infrastructure moves slowly and is measured with error.

  • The levels estimates are several times the published 0.07 to 0.10 and are in the region of Aschauer’s 0.39. Adding physical capital and schooling cuts them by about two thirds.
  • Annual changes give almost nothing, because a year is too short for infrastructure to show up in output and the noise swamps the signal. Lengthening the horizon recovers a positive number.
  • No row reproduces the paper’s 0.07 to 0.10 exactly. The paper uses a pooled mean group estimator that models short-run dynamics and imposes one long-run slope. The simple rows bracket it.
  • None of these rows proves that infrastructure causes growth. Rich countries could build more infrastructure because they are growing, and every row here is open to that objection.
