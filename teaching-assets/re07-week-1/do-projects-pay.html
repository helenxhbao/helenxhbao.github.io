Do large transport projects pay?

Ansar, Flyvbjerg, Budzier and Lunn (2016) collected the outcomes of 95 road and rail projects built in China between 1984 and 2008. Planners expected each to return about 1.4 to 1.5 times its cost. Costs ran over in three projects in four, and traffic fell short in two in three. This page draws portfolios of projects from the distributions the paper reports, and shows that a single project can turn out almost any way while a portfolio of dozens behaves in a predictable and disappointing way.

RE07 Global Infrastructure Investment and Management, Week 1. Source: Ansar, A., Flyvbjerg, B., Budzier, A. and Lunn, D. (2016), “Does infrastructure investment lead to economic growth or economic fragility? Evidence from China”, Oxford Review of Economic Policy 32(3), 360–390. The paper’s project-level data are not published, so the page does not use them. It uses the paper’s published summary statistics (Table 3 and the text of section V) as the parameters of the draws, and assumes that cost and benefit outcomes are independent. It is an illustration of what those statistics imply and not a re-analysis of the data.

  • The paper’s own project-level data would show how cost and benefit outcomes move together. Here they are independent, and the close match to the published shares suggests that the assumption does little harm.
  • A project that falls short does not always fail. A project with a high appraisal ratio and a modest overrun still pays, which is why the shares move so much with the first slider.
  • The last statistic is a reference class adjustment. It is the appraisal ratio at which the median project would break even once the paper’s average pattern of overruns and shortfalls is applied, and it is the kind of figure a group project should compute for a real project.
