Assets against liabilities

A pension fund does not care about the return on its assets in isolation. It cares about the gap between its assets and what it owes, the surplus. An asset that moves with the liability is valuable for that reason alone, whatever its own risk. Sharpe and Tint (1990) put this into mean-variance form, and Keel and Müller (1995) solve it in closed form. Use it to see when a long-dated, inflation-linked asset earns a place in the portfolio, and how much illiquidity the investor can bear before it stops paying.

What the model says

Let RA be the return on assets and RL the growth of the liability. With a funding ratio f0 the return on the surplus is RS = RA − RL/f0. The investor maximises γ E[RS] − Var[RS]/2. Keel and Müller show that the efficient portfolios are the minimum-risk portfolio plus γ times a return-seeking component, x* = xMIN + γz*, and that the liability shifts the minimum-risk portfolio by a component that does not depend on the investor's risk tolerance (equations 11 and 12, page 39). An asset that is highly correlated with the liability earns a large weight in xMIN even if its own return is modest.

That is the case for a long-dated, inflation-linked asset, and the second chart shows how fragile it is. The weight in infrastructure and the return it can give up both climb as its correlation with the liability rises, and both fall away if the link is weak. The second chart also needs a caution that the model leaves out. Andonov, Kräussl and Rauh (2021) find that closed infrastructure funds carry risk much like other private funds and have cash flows that rise and fall with the market (page 3883), which is the case of a high correlation with equities and a low one with the liability. The page treats illiquidity as a return give-up. The break-even is the largest give-up at which the investor still prefers to hold the asset.
