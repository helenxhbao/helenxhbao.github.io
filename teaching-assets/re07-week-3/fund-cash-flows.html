The cash flows of a closed-end fund

A limited partner commits money to a fund, but the fund calls it over several years, and pays it back over several more. In between, the investor's net position is negative before it turns positive: the J-curve. The Yale model of Takahashi and Alexander (2002) turns that pattern into three simple equations. Set the inputs, and see how a commitment becomes exposure, how deep the J is, and how long it takes to recover.

What the model says

The model has three equations. Capital called in year t is the rate of contribution times the commitment not yet called, Ct = RCt (CC − PICt−1). Distributions are a share of the grown asset value, Dt = RDt NAVt−1(1+G), where RDt = max[Y, (t/L)B]. And the value of the fund is its previous value grown, plus calls, less distributions. Calls are front-loaded and distributions back-loaded, so the investor is a net payer for the first years and a net receiver after. That is the J.

The model teaches liquidity planning, not return. A commitment is not an exposure. The first year the investor holds only a quarter of it, and the exposure peaks in the middle of the life. An investor that wants a steady exposure has to commit every year, and the third chart shows how long it takes a programme to reach a stable size and what it needs in cash meanwhile. Real funds also charge fees, write assets up and down, and call capital to pay fees, none of which is in the model, so real J-curves are deeper than the one here.
