Fees and carried interest as claims on a fund

A fund manager is paid in two ways. The management fee is fixed and arrives whatever the fund earns. Carried interest, a share of the profit, arrives only if the fund does well, so it is a claim with the shape of an option. Metrick and Yasuda (2010) value both, and find that most of what a manager expects to earn comes from the fixed part. Set the terms and see what each part is worth, and what the fund has to earn to pay for them.

What the model says

The manager's carry is paid on profit, so it is worth something only if the fund's proceeds exceed the carry basis, here the committed capital. That is the payoff of a call option with the basis as its strike, and the carry level is the fraction of the option the manager owns. A hurdle moves the strike up, and a catch-up lets the manager recover what the hurdle withheld once the fund clears it. Like any option, the claim gains from volatility, which is why a manager paid mainly in carry has a reason to take risk, and why the second chart slopes upward.

Limited partners will commit only if they expect to earn their cost of capital after the manager is paid. The page therefore asks what the fund has to earn, over and above the return on the money invested, for investors to break even. That excess is the present value of the fees plus the present value of the carry. Metrick and Yasuda find that about 60 per cent of the manager's expected revenue is fixed (working paper, abstract). The default terms here give a fixed share near 70 per cent, in the same range, and a fund with its performance fee waived has a fixed share of 100 per cent, so its manager is paid for managing and not for performing. The Week 1 page on fee drag asks the matching question from the investor's side.

The model prices the carry as if the fund's assets could be replicated and then adds the excess return that investors need, which is a simplification of the equilibrium argument in the paper. The fee is charged on committed capital for the whole life, which overstates fees for funds that step down after the investment period.
