Search, dealers and the bid–ask spread: what does immediacy cost?

An unlisted asset does not trade on an exchange. An owner who wants to sell has to find a buyer, and the buyer has to find the owner. Duffie, Gârleanu and Pedersen (2005) built the simplest model of such a market, in which investors meet one another at random and a dealer, when found, will buy at a bid and sell at an ask. The price of the asset falls below the value of its income because waiting is costly, and the dealer's spread is the part of that cost that an owner pays to avoid waiting longer. Set how easily investors find one another and a dealer, and how much bargaining power the dealer has, and see the prices, the spread and the waiting time.

What the model says

Investors hold at most one unit of an asset that pays 1 a year. Some are of a high type and enjoy the asset fully. Others are of a low type and bear a holding cost, so they would like to sell, and the high types who do not own it would like to buy. Types change at random. A trade between a low-type owner and a high-type non-owner is worth making, but only if they meet, and meetings are random. A dealer who is found can take either side, and lays off the position at once on an inter-dealer market, so the dealer holds no inventory and earns only the spread. The price an owner can expect is the present value of the income, 1/r, less an illiquidity discount that grows with the cost of holding and with the time it takes to find a counterparty.

Three results are worth taking from the page. First, the discount is much larger than the dealer's spread. Most of what illiquidity costs is the cost of waiting, which a dealer shortens but does not remove, and the spread is only the price of having it shortened. Second, the spread falls as investors find one another or a dealer more easily, because each investor's outside option, the next counterparty, improves, but only when the dealer has less than all of the bargaining power. Third, with a monopolist dealer (100 per cent) the spread rises with the dealer's reach, because a dealer who is found more often can charge for it. The comparison with brokerage and arranging is direct: an adviser or arranger is paid for shortening a search, and the price of that service depends on how many alternatives the client has.

Every investor has linear utility and holds one unit or none. There is no price impact, no risk aversion, no information problem and no entry of dealers, and the dealer has no inventory cost, which is why the spread is so small. The paper's later sections add risk-averse investors and endogenous search. An unlisted infrastructure stake has far fewer potential buyers than any setting on this page, and the preset for it is a guess at the order of magnitude and not an estimate.
