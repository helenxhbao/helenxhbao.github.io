Certification, liability and competition: what makes an approval worth having?

A rating agency, a lead arranger, a technical adviser and an auditor all sell the same thing: an approval that others rely on. A certifier can do the checking properly or can say yes to anyone who pays, and the buyer of a certificate, who wants the approval and not the checking, prefers the second. What disciplines the certifier is its exposure if a wrong approval comes to light. Franzoni (1999) set out a market in which firms buy certificates, certifiers choose how hard to check and compete on the number of certificates they sell, and a liability makes a wrong certificate costly. Set the liability, the number of competing certifiers and the cost of checking, and see how many firms are certified, how good the checking is, and what the whole system is worth.

Equations 1 to 7, Lemma 1 and Proposition 3 are the paper's. The paper's printed formulas for the equilibrium fee and the certifier's profit are hard to read in the copy I have, so I derived the equilibrium from its setup and then checked it by brute force: the demand for certificates was recomputed from the firms' and certifiers' conditions, no certifier could gain by changing its output, and welfare was summed over a grid of 200,000 firms. My expression for the fee in the partial-coverage case is s + (b − s)/(N + 1), which differs from my reading of the printed one, and the paper should be checked. It does agree with the limit the paper states on page 14, a fee that tends to s as the number of certifiers grows. The case in which every firm is certified relies on the paper's assumption that the price is the highest at which demand is one. This is a teaching illustration with generic inputs, not an estimate for any market. It was computed twice, in Python and in this page, and the two agree.

What the model says

A certifier cannot refuse a compliant firm, but it can detect a non-compliant one only by paying for the check. It will check as hard as it can if the expected penalty on a wrong certificate is larger than the cost of checking, and will not check at all if it is smaller. Firms know this. When checking is likely, a non-compliant firm gains little from applying, and some stay out or comply. When the penalty is small, every firm applies, most of them non-compliant, and the certificate is worth little to anyone who relies on it. The certifiers compete on quantity, so the price of a certificate rises above the cost of the check when there are few of them.

Three lessons can be read off the page. First, raising the penalty improves the system by reducing the number of non-compliant firms that try to enter, until the effect fades, because the checking is already as good as it can be and what remains are the firms priced out by the fee. Second, more certifiers lower the fee and raise coverage, and welfare rises quickly at first and then flattens, and may fall slightly, since more firms are checked at a cost and more non-compliant firms are encouraged to apply. Third, the paper warns that a regulator who caps the fee may do harm, because a lower fee lets more firms apply and with them more of the non-compliant ones. Cheap approval is not good approval, and the market for it has to be disciplined by liability. For investment banks, rating agencies and arrangers, the question is who bears the loss when an approval proves wrong, and how likely it is that anyone finds out.

There is one kind of firm and one kind of certifier, and the penalty is a fixed sum paid when a wrong certificate is discovered. Certifiers are identical and cannot differentiate their quality or their price. Reputation, which in practice may do more than liability to discipline a certifier, is not in the model. A certifier that is also the lender, as an arranger is, has an interest in the outcome that this model does not capture. The paper's own policy analysis, from page 17, covers price regulation as well, which this page does not reproduce.
