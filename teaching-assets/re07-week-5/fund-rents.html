Skill, size and fees: why do investors pay for managers who do not beat the market for them?

An infrastructure fund, a private credit fund or a mutual fund charges a fee and reports a return. Investors chase the managers who have done well, and yet the managers who did well do not do better next time, and investors as a group earn nothing above the benchmark after fees. Berk and Green (2004) showed that all three facts are what a rational market of skilled and unskilled managers would produce. Capital flows in until the last dollar earns nothing for investors, so the fruits of skill go to the manager and to the costs of managing a bigger fund. Set the skill in the industry, the cost of size and the fee, and watch a population of funds grow, learn and level off.

What the model says

Managers differ in skill, which no one can observe; investors and managers alike learn it from the returns. Running a larger fund costs more per dollar because trades move prices. Investors compare the fund with the benchmark and will put money in until the return they expect after costs and fees is zero, which happens when the unit cost of active management plus the fee equals the skill the fund is believed to have. A fund that has done well is believed to be more skilled, so money flows in and its size rises until investors are again indifferent. The flow is a response to performance, and larger for young funds, whose records are short and so move beliefs more.

Because investors have already moved until they expect nothing, last year's winner is no more likely than anyone else to beat the benchmark for them next year, and the average net return to investors is zero whatever the average skill is. That is what the second chart shows. The skill is real, and it earns a rent, but the rent goes to the manager, who is paid the fee on a fund that skill has made large, and to the cost of managing it. The evidence therefore cannot be read as showing that managers are unskilled, and the response to performance cannot be read as irrational. Berk and van Binsbergen (2012) measure the dollars a manager adds, the fund's size times its gross excess return, and find that skill is positive and persists for years even though the net return to investors does not.

For infrastructure, the model gives a way to ask where the fees come from and what limits the growth of funds. It does not say that fees of an infrastructure fund are earned. It says that, in a competitive market for capital, a fee equal to the manager's marginal contribution is compatible with investors earning only their benchmark, and that what to look for is the cost of size: whether the best managers can deploy more capital without a fall in return, and whether capital is as free to move as the model assumes. Unlisted funds lock capital in for ten years, which the model rules out.

The model is partial equilibrium, with a benchmark that no one can move, and costs that depend on size only. Investors can supply capital without limit to any fund with positive expected excess return, and take it out at no cost. None of this holds for a closed-end infrastructure fund, and the page should be read as a statement about what competition among investors implies, not as a description of private markets.
