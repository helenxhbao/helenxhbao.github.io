The Economic Transformation Database uses its own construction, financial services and real estate groups on the same lines.

Why a property boom can pull workers into real estate

Hsieh and Moretti (2003) study the real estate brokerage industry. Commissions are a fixed percentage of the sale price, so when land prices rise each sale becomes more lucrative and more brokers enter. But each broker then sells fewer houses per hour, and the real wage of a typical broker does not change. The extra workers add to the cost of selling a house, not to output. Murphy, Shleifer and Vishny (1991) give the wider argument: when the returns to activities that redistribute wealth are high, talent flows there rather than into producing goods, and growth suffers.

What to look for

  • A sector whose share of new jobs is far above its share of existing jobs is absorbing labour faster than the rest of the economy. Compare the bar with the vertical line.
  • The boom economies. Compare the United States with Spain, Ireland or the United Kingdom over 2000 to mid-2006, then over mid-2006 to 2019.
  • Poorer economies. Construction’s role as an employer differs sharply when the economy is urbanising, as in China, or still mainly agricultural, as in Ethiopia.

Reading the numbers with care

  • A share of net job additions can exceed 100% or be negative: if one sector loses jobs, others must add more than the total. When total employment barely changes, shares swing widely, so use net jobs.
  • How this page relates to slide 6. The slide does not follow the measure in Hsieh and Moretti (2003), who study the share of real estate brokers in each US city’s workforce, not national job additions. Its chart restates a Credit Suisse exhibit of the share of US job additions coming from “real estate”, which the exhibit did not define. The closest reproduction from Bureau of Labor Statistics data adds construction to financial activities, a published aggregate that contains finance, insurance and real estate, because the BLS publishes real estate on its own only from 1990. Finance is therefore in the slide as a proxy for the exhibit, and it overstates real estate proper. This page uses the same three groups (construction, finance and insurance, real estate) for its red bars and headline figures, and also lets you view construction and real estate alone, or any one sector.
  • Sectors are harmonised into ten groups across three sources. “Real estate” is a small, separate sector in every source and is not the same thing as property-related work as a whole: construction, finance and business services also serve the property market. “Trade, transport and hospitality” includes communication in the ETD. The United States uses the slide’s own endpoints (December to December, with June 2006 as the breakpoint) and reproduces the slide’s last three periods exactly (28.5%, 1.8% and 16.7%). Other economies use annual averages for the start and end years, so comparisons are close but not exact. The slide’s earlier periods (1971 to 1999) cannot be shown here because the BLS publishes finance and real estate separately only from 1990.
  • US figures count nonfarm payroll jobs and have no agriculture. Eurostat counts employed persons. The ETD counts persons employed, including the self-employed and informal workers.
