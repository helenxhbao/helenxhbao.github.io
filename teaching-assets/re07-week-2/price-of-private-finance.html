The price of private finance

The government can borrow at the gilt rate; a privately financed project company borrows at more. The difference is the price of private finance, and the bundled operating contract has to earn it back. This page asks how large a saving on operating costs a privately financed project must deliver to match a publicly financed one, using the cost-of-capital figures reported by the National Audit Office.

RE07 Global Infrastructure Investment and Management, Week 2. Financing arithmetic and figures: National Audit Office (2018), PFI and PF2, HC 718, Part One, pages 14 to 16. The comparison is a public sector comparator in the form the NAO describes, with both cost streams valued at the government’s own cost of funds. It is a teaching illustration and not an appraisal of any project, and it was computed in Python and in this page, which agree. What the calculation says

Private finance costs more for reasons the NAO sets out: a higher cost of debt and equity than the government pays, an arrangement fee, insurance and cash reserves that the Treasury would not hold, advisers, and the costs of the project company (paragraphs 1.17 and 1.18). The 2010 National Infrastructure Plan put the premium at 2 to 3.75 per cent over gilts, and deals since 2013 forecast 2 to 4 per cent, although four social housing deals of 2013 projected over 8 per cent, more than 5 per cent above the cost of government borrowing at the time (paragraph 1.17). Small differences compound: £100 repaid over 30 years costs £34 in interest at 2 per cent and £73 at 4 per cent.

The bundled contract has to repay the difference. Hart (2003) gives the reason it might: a builder who also operates has a reason to design for lower running costs. The NAO finds that the evidence for this is thin. Its survey found no evidence of operational efficiency in PFI hospitals, and savings on operational contracts have been hard to achieve (paragraphs 1.9 and 2.7), while the Department for Education estimated PF2 schools at about 40 per cent more cash than a publicly financed comparator, and the Treasury Committee put a privately financed hospital at 70 per cent more in 2011 (paragraph 1.19). This page converts that into the saving that the bundle would have to deliver.
