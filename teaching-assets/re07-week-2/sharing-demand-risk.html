Sharing demand risk in a concession

A toll road earns whatever the traffic allows, and nobody knows the traffic. A concessionaire who bears all of that risk will demand a high price for doing so; a government that bears all of it must raise taxes, which are costly too. Engel, Fischer and Galetovic (2013) derive the contract that shares the risk at least cost: a minimum revenue guarantee, a revenue cap, and a concession term that flexes in between. What the model says

The government prefers to pay the concessionaire from user fees, because a pound of subsidy costs more than a pound of fees (ζ > 0). The firm, however, dislikes risk, so it wants insurance against low demand, and the only way to give it is a subsidy. The optimal contract splits the states of demand into three. In low states (v < m) the concession runs indefinitely and the government tops receipts up to the guarantee m. In intermediate states the concession also runs indefinitely and no subsidy is paid. In high states (v > M) the concession ends as soon as the firm has collected M in present value, and the government takes the rest (Proposition 2, NBER working paper page 15). The gap between the cap and the guarantee is set by how much the firm dislikes risk and how wasteful subsidies are: with exponential utility, M − m = ln(1+ζ)/ρ.

Two conclusions matter for the module. First, a PPP does not relieve a strained budget: savings on the investment now are given back as user fees forgone later (pages 2 to 3, and Table 1 on page 41). Second, the contract that fits the risk is a long way from the guarantees and revenue-sharing clauses seen in practice, which the authors say bear no relation to the optimum (page 6).
