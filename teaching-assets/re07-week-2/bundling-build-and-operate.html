Bundling build and operate: Hart's incomplete-contract model

A government can contract with one firm to build a prison and with another to run it (conventional provision), or with one firm to build and run it (a public-private partnership). Hart (2003) shows that the choice turns on what the contract can specify. Move the sliders and the switches to see which arrangement delivers more, and why.

1. What the builder can do

Two investments that the contract cannot verify. Each costs the builder one unit per unit of investment.

Investment i (a better-designed, easier-to-run building) cuts operating cost by 2Ψ√i. A builder who also runs the asset keeps this saving.

The same investment raises the benefit of the service by 2Β√i. No builder is paid for it, whoever runs the asset.

Investment e (the electric fence that needs fewer guards) cuts operating cost by γe − e²/2.

The same cut lowers the benefit by δe + e²/2. Hart assumes γ − δ ≤ 1, so shading is never socially worth doing.

What the model says

Under conventional provision the builder is paid a fixed price to build and is indifferent to what the operator will later pay, so he builds the cheapest building the contract allows: i = e = 0. He under-invests in the building but, by luck, does not cut corners. Under a PPP the builder bears the operating cost, so he invests in the better building until the marginal saving equals the marginal cost (ψ′(i) = 1), which is more than before but still too little because he is not paid for the benefit to society. The same incentive makes him cut corners until the marginal saving falls to the marginal cost (c′(e) = 1), which is too much because the lost quality is not his cost.

Hart's conclusion follows. Conventional provision is better when the building can be specified but the service cannot, because the under-investment in i matters less than the corner-cutting a PPP invites. A PPP is better when the service can be specified but the building cannot. Hart's examples are prisons and schools, where the building is simple to specify and the service is not, and hospitals, where it may be the other way round (pages 9 to 10).

Hart also notes (pages 10 to 11) that the conclusion is at odds with the usual case for PPPs, that the private sector finances or insures more cheaply. The government has the power to tax and so is hard to beat as a borrower or an insurer, and he suggests attention should shift from financing to the relative cost of writing contracts. Engel, Fischer and Galetovic (2013) take up the financing point, and the third page in this set measures it.
